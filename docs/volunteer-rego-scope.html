Who we are designing for

Meet Margaret

My grandson is running, so I thought I'd help out. I just want to know where to stand, what time, and who to ring if I get stuck.

Margaret is a made-up volunteer, but you have met her at every aid station. She is 70. She has an iPad on the kitchen bench and a phone she mostly uses for calls, texts, photos and Facebook. She reads with her glasses on and taps with one careful finger.

She is happy to give up a Saturday. She is not happy to create an account, invent a password, or scroll a spreadsheet of shift codes. If the first screen looks like hard work, she closes it and tells her grandson she tried.

If the platform works for Margaret, it works for everyone. That is the design rule for this whole scope.

The Margaret test

  1. Look before you sign in.She can see every job, where it is and when, before giving a single detail.
  2. One question per screen.Big words, big buttons, plain English. "Hand out drinks", never "AS2 Vol Shift B".
  3. Show me, don't tell me.Photos of the job and a picture of where it is. She should recognise it when she arrives.
  4. No password, ever.Her name, her mobile and a link sent to her are enough to get back in.
  5. A human on every screen.A phone number she can ring, and a team member who can finish the sign-up for her.
  6. Tell me what I said yes to.Straight away: the job, the place, the time, what to bring and who her contact is.
  7. Easy to change my mind.One tap to swap or pull out, with no guilt and no hunting through menus.
  8. Ask for less, explain why.Only the details the job needs, each with a plain reason beside it.
What hurts today

Why iVolunteer has to go

Hard to find a role

Volunteers can't easily see what jobs exist, where they are on course, or which one suits them. They pick blind or they give up.

Clunky on a phone

Too much text, small buttons and nothing visual. Most people open the sign-up link on a phone, so that is where we lose them.

Painful for the team

Setting up events, filling gaps, moving people between shifts, messaging and reporting all take too long for the Event Management Team.

What we want in its place: intuitive, visual, easy to find what you want, fun, and safe with people's details. Volunteers are the heart of every SingleTrack event. Signing up should feel like the start of a good day out, not a form.

What the platform covers

The scope: the whole volunteer journey

Five stages, from first look to thank-you. Each stage has a volunteer side and a team side, and both are in scope.

STAGE 1

Find

Volunteer

  • See every event and every job without signing in
  • Photos, plain job names, place and time
  • See what is nearly full and what needs help most

Team

  • Set up an event from last year's template
  • Write a job once, reuse it across events
STAGE 2

Sign up

Volunteer

  • Name, mobile, email and an emergency contact
  • Sign up with a friend, a family or a running club
  • Instant confirmation on screen and by message

Team

  • Sign someone up over the phone on their behalf
  • Caps and waitlists for each job
STAGE 3

Get ready

Volunteer

  • Reminders a week out and the day before
  • One page with place, time, parking, what to bring and the induction handbook
  • Swap or pull out with one tap

Team

  • Live roster with gaps showing
  • Message one job, one aid station or everyone
STAGE 4

Race day

Volunteer

  • Check in with a tap or have their name ticked off
  • Their contact for the day and how to reach Event HQ

Team

  • Who has arrived, by aid station and job
  • Printable roster as a backup
  • Emergency contacts on hand for the people who need them
STAGE 5

Afterwards

Volunteer

  • A proper thank-you with photos from the day
  • Rewards and credits recorded for them
  • First look at next year's jobs

Team

  • Hours, numbers and no-shows by event
  • Feedback gathered in one place

Keeping people's details safe

  • Collect only what the job needs: name, mobile, email, emergency contact, and anything a specific role requires.
  • Sign-in by a link or code sent to the volunteer, so there are no passwords to steal or forget.
  • Details stored in Australia and handled under the Privacy Act and the Australian Privacy Principles.
  • Each team member sees only what their role needs. Emergency contacts are visible to the Event Management Team for that event.
  • Two-step sign-in for every team account.
  • A plain-English privacy page: what we keep, why, for how long, and how to have it deleted.
  • Details are removed after a set period of inactivity, and on request.
  • A written plan for what happens if something goes wrong, with a named owner.

For later, after launch

  • Linking volunteer credits straight into runner entries.
  • Paid crew and contractor rostering.
  • A phone app in the app stores. A good web page that works on any phone comes first.
  • Languages other than English.

Built for everyone

  • Text at least 18px, strong contrast, buttons a thumb can't miss.
  • Works on an old phone, an iPad and a slow connection in Halls Gap.
  • Works with screen readers and meets WCAG 2.2 AA.
Five ways to do it

Five platform ideas

Each idea pairs a volunteer experience with the build route that suits it. The screens are rough sketches to show the feel, with example jobs and times. Margaret has her say on each one.

Idea 1

The Trail Map

Every event opens on an illustrated course map. Tap the village, an aid station or a summit and see the jobs there, the times, a photo of the spot and how many places are left. The team sees the same map with gaps glowing, so filling the roster is as visual as signing up.

Build route
Custom web build, map first
Time to first event
Longer
Running cost
Medium, plus map artwork per event

Pros

  • The most visual option. It answers "where will I be?" before anything else.
  • Shows off the locations, which is the reason people love these events.
  • The team spots unfilled positions at a glance.
  • Nothing else in volunteering looks like it. It would be ours.

Cons

  • Maps are fiddly on a small phone, and harder again for unsteady hands.
  • Plenty of people don't read maps. It needs a plain list beside it.
  • Each event needs its own map drawn and kept up to date when courses change.
  • Remote pins can put first-timers off unless travel time and facilities are spelled out.

Idea 2

Three Easy Questions

A friendly helper asks three things: which event, when are you free, and what sort of job sounds good. It then offers the three best matches as big cards. Pick one, add your name and mobile, done. Behind the scenes it steers people towards the jobs that most need filling.

Build route
Low-code: form, database and automations
Time to first event
Quickest
Running cost
Low monthly fees

Pros

  • One decision per screen. Nobody gets lost.
  • Matches people to jobs they can do comfortably, including seated and low-walking roles.
  • "Put me where I'm needed" quietly fills the hard positions.
  • Fast and cheap to stand up, so we can test it with real volunteers early.

Cons

  • You can't browse everything. People who want a specific aid station need another way in.
  • Regulars will find three questions slow the fifth time around.
  • Low-code tools set limits on look, feel and where details are stored.
  • The team side is basic. Rostering and race-day check-in would be bolted on.

Margaret says

"Well, that was easy. It asked me, I answered, and it found me a chair at the finish line. Why isn't everything like this?"

Idea 3

The Noticeboard

Like the board at the community hall. Every job is a big photo card with a plain name, the time, the place and the spots left. A few chunky filters narrow it down: day, sitting or standing, near the village. Tap a card, tap "count me in".

Build route
Buy a better off-the-shelf product and style it
Time to first event
Quick
Running cost
Subscription, grows with volunteer numbers

Pros

  • Familiar. Anyone who has shopped online knows how cards and filters work.
  • The team tools come ready-made: rostering, messaging, check-in and reports.
  • The supplier looks after security, hosting and support.
  • Lowest delivery risk. The product already works for other events.

Cons

  • We get their sign-up flow. It may end up only a little better than iVolunteer.
  • Limited say over accounts and passwords, which is the Margaret deal-breaker.
  • Where details are stored and how we get them out again depends on the supplier.
  • Map views and the fun touches are unlikely to be on offer.

"I like the photos. I can see what I'd be doing. But then it wanted a password with a capital letter and a symbol, and I went and put the kettle on."

Idea 4

Text Me a Link

One short sign-up page on the SingleTrack website, then everything else arrives by text and email: confirmation, reminders, parking, what to bring. Reply YES to confirm or SWAP to change. A personal link opens "my volunteering" with no sign-in. Anyone who would rather talk can ring, and the team signs them up on the spot.

Build route
Add-on to the existing website plus a messaging service
Time to first event
Quick
Running cost
Low, plus a fee per text

Pros

  • Meets volunteers where they already are. Everyone can read a text.
  • Nothing to remember and nothing to install.
  • Reminders with a one-word reply cut no-shows and tell the team early about gaps.
  • Holds very little personal detail, so there is less to protect.

Cons

  • Older volunteers are rightly told to distrust links in texts. Messages must be clearly ours.
  • The least visual and least fun of the five.
  • Browsing jobs happens somewhere else, so finding a role is only half solved.
  • Someone has to handle the replies that aren't YES or SWAP.

Margaret says

"A text I can manage. Though my daughter says never click on links, so I'd want to know it's really you."

Idea 5

The Crew Club

A home for SingleTrack volunteers. A passport that collects a stamp for every event crewed, rewards that build up, photos from the day, and one-tap sign-up for regulars. Friends, families and running clubs sign up as a team. For the Event Management Team it is the full kit: templates, rostering, messaging, race-day check-in and reporting in one place.

Build route
Full custom platform, built in phases
Time to first event
Longest
Running cost
Highest, with ongoing upkeep

Pros

  • The most fun, and the best at bringing volunteers back year after year.
  • Regulars sign up in one tap. The platform already knows them.
  • Covers the whole journey for volunteers and for the team.
  • SingleTrack owns the experience and the data, with nobody else's limits.

Cons

  • The biggest and slowest build, and someone has to maintain it for years.
  • Holds the most personal detail, so the security job is the largest.
  • The payoff comes on a volunteer's third event, not their first.
  • Stamps and rewards can feel childish if overdone. They need a light touch.

Margaret says

"Stamps! Like my coffee card. I'd want all eleven. But I only got here because someone helped me join the first time."

Where we'd start

Our pick: blend the best three, then grow

Ask three questions, show the noticeboard, confirm by text.

No single idea wins on its own. The front door from Idea 2 gets Margaret in. The photo cards from Idea 3 let everyone else browse. The messages and password-free link from Idea 4 keep her informed and get her back in. Build that as our own lean web app, so we keep control of the sign-in and of where details are stored, then add the map and the club once the basics are proven.

Phase 1: the basics, done beautifullyThree questions, photo cards, password-free sign-up, reminders, a live roster and messaging for the team. Pilot at one smaller Running Wild event.
Phase 2: the map and race dayTrail Map view for the big multi-location events, race-day check-in, printable rosters and reporting.
Phase 3: the Crew ClubPassport stamps, rewards, team sign-ups and one-tap sign-up for regulars.

Before committing to a build, spend a fortnight putting two or three off-the-shelf products through the Margaret test. If one passes with password-free sign-up and Australian data storage, buying could beat building for Phase 1.
