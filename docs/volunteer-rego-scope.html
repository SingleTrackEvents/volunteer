Who we are designing for

Meet Margaret

My grandson is running, so I thought I'd help out. I just want to know where to stand, what time, and who to ring if I get stuck.

Margaret is a made-up volunteer, but you have met her at every aid station. She is 70. She has an iPad on the kitchen bench and a phone she mostly uses for calls, texts, photos and Facebook. She reads with her glasses on and taps with one careful finger.

She is happy to give up a Saturday. She is not happy to create an account, invent a password, or scroll a spreadsheet of shift codes. If the first screen looks like hard work, she closes it and tells her grandson she tried.

If the platform works for Margaret, it works for everyone. That is the design rule for this whole scope.

The Margaret test

  1. Look before you sign in.She can see every job, where it is and when, before giving a single detail.
  2. One question per screen.Big words, big buttons, plain English. "Hand out drinks", never "AS2 Vol Shift B".
  3. Show me, don't tell me.Photos of the job and a picture of where it is. She should recognise it when she arrives.
  4. No password, ever.Her name, her mobile and a link sent to her are enough to get back in.
  5. A human on every screen.A phone number she can ring, and a team member who can finish the sign-up for her.
  6. Tell me what I said yes to.Straight away: the job, the place, the time, what to bring and who her contact is.
  7. Easy to change my mind.One tap to swap or pull out, with no guilt and no hunting through menus.
  8. Ask for less, explain why.Only the details the job needs, each with a plain reason beside it.
What hurts today

Why iVolunteer has to go

Hard to find a role

Volunteers can't easily see what jobs exist, where they are on course, or which one suits them. They pick blind or they give up.

Clunky on a phone

Too much text, small buttons and nothing visual. Most people open the sign-up link on a phone, so that is where we lose them.

Painful for the team

Setting up events, filling gaps, moving people between shifts, messaging and reporting all take too long for the Event Management Team.

What we want in its place: intuitive, visual, easy to find what you want, fun, and safe with people's details. Volunteers are the heart of every SingleTrack event. Signing up should feel like the start of a good day out, not a form.

What the platform covers

The scope: the whole volunteer journey

Five stages, from first look to thank-you. Each stage has a volunteer side and a team side, and both are in scope.

STAGE 1

Find

Volunteer

  • See every event and every job without signing in
  • Photos, plain job names, place and time
  • See what is nearly full and what needs help most

Team

  • Set up an event from last year's template
  • Write a job once, reuse it across events
STAGE 2

Sign up

Volunteer

  • Name, mobile, email and an emergency contact
  • Sign up with a friend, a family or a running club
  • Instant confirmation on screen and by message

Team

  • Sign someone up over the phone on their behalf
  • Caps and waitlists for each job
STAGE 3

Get ready

Volunteer

  • Reminders a week out and the day before
  • One page with place, time, parking, what to bring and the induction handbook
  • Swap or pull out with one tap

Team

  • Live roster with gaps showing
  • Message one job, one aid station or everyone
STAGE 4

Race day

Volunteer

  • Check in with a tap or have their name ticked off
  • Their contact for the day and how to reach Event HQ

Team

  • Who has arrived, by aid station and job
  • Printable roster as a backup
  • Emergency contacts on hand for the people who need them
STAGE 5

Afterwards

Volunteer

  • A proper thank-you with photos from the day
  • Rewards and credits recorded for them
  • First look at next year's jobs

Team

  • Hours, numbers and no-shows by event
  • Feedback gathered in one place

Keeping people's details safe

  • Collect only what the job needs: name, mobile, email, emergency contact, and anything a specific role requires.
  • Sign-in by a link or code sent to the volunteer, so there are no passwords to steal or forget.
  • Details stored in Australia and handled under the Privacy Act and the Australian Privacy Principles.
  • Each team member sees only what their role needs. Emergency contacts are visible to the Event Management Team for that event.
  • Two-step sign-in for every team account.
  • A plain-English privacy page: what we keep, why, for how long, and how to have it deleted.
  • Details are removed after a set period of inactivity, and on request.
  • A written plan for what happens if something goes wrong, with a named owner.

For later, after launch

  • Linking volunteer credits straight into runner entries.
  • Paid crew and contractor rostering.
  • A phone app in the app stores. A good web page that works on any phone comes first.
  • Languages other than English.

Built for everyone

  • Text at least 18px, strong contrast, buttons a thumb can't miss.
  • Works on an old phone, an iPad and a slow connection in Halls Gap.
  • Works with screen readers and meets WCAG 2.2 AA.
Five ways to do it

Five platform ideas

Each idea pairs a volunteer experience with the build route that suits it. The screens are rough sketches to show the feel, with example jobs and times. Margaret has her say on each one.

Idea 1

The Trail Map

Every event opens on an illustrated course map. Tap the village, an aid station or a summit and see the jobs there, the times, a photo of the spot and how many places are left. The team sees the same map with gaps glowing, so filling the roster is as visual as signing up.

Build route
Custom web build, map first
Time to first event
Longer
Running cost
Medium, plus map artwork per event

Pros

  • The most visual option. It answers "where will I be?" before anything else.
  • Shows off the locations, which is the reason people love these events.
  • The team spots unfilled positions at a glance.
  • Nothing else in volunteering looks like it. It would be ours.

Cons

  • Maps are fiddly on a small phone, and harder again for unsteady hands.
  • Plenty of people don't read maps. It needs a plain list beside it.
  • Each event needs its own map drawn and kept up to date when courses change.
  • Remote pins can put first-timers off unless travel time and facilities are spelled out.

Idea 2

Three Easy Questions

A friendly helper asks three things: which event, when are you free, and what sort of job sounds good. It then offers the three best matches as big cards. Pick one, add your name and mobile, done. Behind the scenes it steers people towards the jobs that most need filling.

Build route
Low-code: form, database and automations
Time to first event
Quickest
Running cost
Low monthly fees

Pros

  • One decision per screen. Nobody gets lost.
  • Matches people to jobs they can do comfortably, including seated and low-walking roles.
  • "Put me where I'm needed" quietly fills the hard positions.
  • Fast and cheap to stand up, so we can test it with real volunteers early.

Cons

  • You can't browse everything. People who want a specific aid station need another way in.
  • Regulars will find three questions slow the fifth time around.
  • Low-code tools set limits on look, feel and where details are stored.
  • The team side is basic. Rostering and race-day check-in would be bolted on.

Margaret says

"Well, that was easy. It asked me, I answered, and it found me a chair at the finish line. Why isn't everything like this?"

Idea 3

The Noticeboard

Like the board at the community hall. Every job is a big photo card with a plain name, the time, the place and the spots left. A few chunky filters narrow it down: day, sitting or standing, near the village. Tap a card, tap "count me in".

Build route
Buy a better off-the-shelf product and style it
Time to first event
Quick
Running cost
Subscription, grows with volunteer numbers

Pros

  • Familiar. Anyone who has shopped online knows how cards and filters work.
  • The team tools come ready-made: rostering, messaging, check-in and reports.
  • The supplier looks after security, hosting and support.
  • Lowest delivery risk. The product already works for other events.

Cons

  • We get their sign-up flow. It may end up only a little better than iVolunteer.
  • Limited say over accounts and passwords, which is the Margaret deal-breaker.
  • Where details are stored and how we get them out again depends on the supplier.
  • Map views and the fun touches are unlikely to be on offer.

Idea 4

Text Me a Link

One short sign-up page on the SingleTrack website, then everything else arrives by text and email: confirmation, reminders, parking, what to bring. Reply YES to confirm or SWAP to change. A personal link opens "my volunteering" with no sign-in. Anyone who would rather talk can ring, and the team signs them up on the spot.

Build route
Add-on to the existing website plus a messaging service
Time to first event
Quick
Running cost
Low, plus a fee per text

Pros

  • Meets volunteers where they already are. Everyone can read a text.
  • Nothing to remember and nothing to install.
  • Reminders with a one-word reply cut no-shows and tell the team early about gaps.
  • Holds very little personal detail, so there is less to protect.

Cons

  • Older volunteers are rightly told to distrust links in texts. Messages must be clearly ours.
  • The least visual and least fun of the five.
  • Browsing jobs happens somewhere else, so finding a role is only half solved.
  • Someone has to handle the replies that aren't YES or SWAP.

"A text I can manage. Though my daughter says never click on links, so I'd want to know it's really you."

Idea 5

The Volunteer Passport

A home for SingleTrack volunteers. A passport that collects a stamp for every event they help at, rewards that build up, photos from the day, and one-tap sign-up for regulars. Friends, families and running clubs sign up as a team. For the Event Management Team it is the full kit: templates, rostering, messaging, race-day check-in and reporting in one place.

Build route
Full custom platform, built in phases
Time to first event
Longest
Running cost
Highest, with ongoing upkeep

Pros

  • The most fun, and the best at bringing volunteers back year after year.
  • Regulars sign up in one tap. The platform already knows them.
  • Covers the whole journey for volunteers and for the team.
  • SingleTrack owns the experience and the data, with nobody else's limits.

Cons

  • The biggest and slowest build, and someone has to maintain it for years.
  • Holds the most personal detail, so the security job is the largest.
  • The payoff comes on a volunteer's third event, not their first.
  • Stamps and rewards can feel childish if overdone. They need a light touch.

Margaret says

"Stamps! Like my coffee card. I'd want all eleven. But I only got here because someone helped me join the first time."

Round two, off the beaten track

Five from left field, ranked by Margaret

The first five ideas all start with a list of jobs. These five start somewhere else: with a person, a runner, a poster, a phone call or a race bib. Each one has been put through the eight-point Margaret test and ranked from best to worst.

✓ passes~ partly✗ failsNumbers match the Margaret test at the top of the page.

Best1

Join Deb's Crew

You don't pick a job. You pick a person. Every aid station and crew has a face: the Aid Station Manager, with a photo and a short hello in their own words. "I'm Deb. I run the aid station at the top of the hill. We have a kettle, a marquee and a good laugh. I need six mates for Saturday morning." Tap "I'm with Deb". Deb sees her crew fill up and welcomes each person herself. Deb is an example name.

1✓2✓3✓4✓5✓6✓7✓8✓ 8 of 8

What works

  • People volunteer for people. A friendly face beats a job description every time.
  • The human on every screen is built in. Her contact on the day is the person she signed up with.
  • Aid Station Managers recruit and look after their own crews, which takes work off the Operations Manager.
  • Crews come back together year after year, because they are a crew.

What trips it up

  • It can become a popularity contest. The big personalities fill first.
  • Aid Station Managers must be locked in early and be happy to have their photo on show.
  • Roles that work alone or in pairs, such as Course Marshals, need a crew and a face of their own.
  • If Deb pulls out, her crew signed up for Deb.

Margaret says

"Oh, she looks nice. And there's a kettle. Put me down with Deb."

Second2

Follow My Runner

The first question is "Who are you here for?" Margaret types her grandson's name and picks his race. The platform draws his day as a simple picture, then offers the jobs that fit around it: "Hand out drinks at the halfway aid station and you'll see Tom come through around 10am. You'll be back at the finish with time to spare." Volunteers with nobody running skip the question and see everything.

1✓2✓3✓4✓5✓6✓7✓8~ 7.5 of 8

What works

  • It starts from the real reason most family members are there, and turns spectators into volunteers.
  • It removes the biggest worry: "If I help, will I miss seeing them?"
  • Every runner becomes a recruiter. "Mum, sign up and you'll see me at halfway."
  • It steers people to on-course jobs that are usually the hardest to fill.

What trips it up

  • Runner times are a guess. If Tom has a shocker or a blinder, Margaret misses him and we broke a promise.
  • It only helps volunteers who have a runner. Everyone else needs the ordinary way in.
  • It asks for someone else's name, so the page must say plainly why and that it isn't kept.
  • Each course needs rough timing worked out for every distance.

Margaret says

"You mean I can help and still see him? Well, why didn't you say so. Halfway it is."

Third3

The Bakery Poster

The sign-up starts on paper, in town. A poster goes up in the bakery, the general store and the footy club, with a row of tear-off tabs along the bottom. Each tab is one job with one word on it: MEDALS, DRINKS, CHEER. Tear one off, text the word to the number on the tab, and you're in. Or ring the number and talk to us. The reply text confirms the job, time and place, and asks for an emergency contact.

1✓2✓3~4✓5✓6✓7✓8✓ 7.5 of 8

What works

  • It reaches the locals in Halls Gap, Bright and Blackheath who never see our emails or socials.
  • She starts the conversation, so there is no worry about a strange link in a text.
  • The tab goes on the fridge as her reminder.
  • Cheap and simple to build: a text number and a list of words.

What trips it up

  • A poster can't update itself. A torn tab is not a sign-up, and a full job still has tabs hanging.
  • Someone has to print, put up and take down posters in each town.
  • The phone side is plain text, so the pictures stop at the poster.
  • It only works where people walk past it. Volunteers from out of town need another way in.

Margaret says

"I saw it while I was getting my vanilla slice. Tore off MEDALS and had my niece show me how to text it. Done before the coffee came."

Fourth4

Just Say When

Margaret doesn't choose a job at all. She gives her name, her mobile and the days she is free, and ticks anything she would rather avoid, such as stairs or early starts. The platform suggests a match and a real person from the team rings or texts with an offer: "We'd love you on finish medals, Saturday 9am. Does that suit?" A week later a card arrives in the post for the fridge, with the job, the time, a little map and who to ring.

1~2✓3~4✓5✓6✓7✓8✓ 7 of 8

What works

  • The shortest sign-up of all ten ideas. There is nothing to choose and nothing to get wrong.
  • The team places people where they are needed, so the hard jobs get filled first.
  • A phone call from a real person is the warmest welcome we can give.
  • The fridge card is a keepsake and a reminder in one.

What trips it up

  • It adds work for the team, and admin is already the sore point. It suits dozens of volunteers, not hundreds.
  • She gives her details before she sees a job, which bends the first rule of the Margaret test.
  • There is a wait between putting her hand up and knowing what she is doing.
  • Printing and posting cards costs a few dollars each and needs a postal address.

Margaret says

"A lovely young man rang and asked if medals would suit. Then a card came in the post. It's on the fridge next to the grandkids."

Worst5

The Volunteer Bib

Volunteering becomes a race you enter. Alongside the 50k and the 20k on the entry page sits a free "Crew" category for each job. Volunteers enter it through runner rego like everyone else. They collect a bib with their name, job and start time printed on it, they get a finisher's medal, and their name goes up in the results with hours crewed in place of a finish time. There is nothing new to build.

1✓2✗3✗4✗5~6✓7✗8✗ 2.5 of 8

What works

  • Volunteers are treated exactly like runners: a bib, a medal and a place in the results.
  • It uses the rego platform SingleTrack already pays for.
  • Runners see the Crew category every time they enter, which is free advertising.
  • Emergency contacts and check-in at bib collection come with it.

What trips it up

  • Runner entry forms are long and built for runners. Margaret is asked for an account, a password and details she doesn't need to give.
  • Swapping or pulling out goes through a race transfer process.
  • There is no rostering, no gap view and no crew messaging for the team.
  • It depends on what the runner rego platform allows, which is still an open item.

Margaret says

"It asked for my estimated finish time and my shirt size. I'm not running, dear. But I would like that medal."

On 16 November 2026 Join Deb's Crew was reshaped: the place and the job lead, and the person appears once confirmed, because places often have nobody leading them when sign-ups open. The Bakery Poster stays on the list as a low-cost extra for the pilot town. From the Volunteer Bib, the bib and the medal are worth keeping for race day. The sign-up is left behind.

Where we'd start

Our pick: blend the best three, then grow

Ask who you're here for, choose where to help, confirm by text.

No single idea wins on its own. The front door from Idea 2 gets Margaret in. The photo cards from Idea 3 let everyone else browse. The messages and password-free link from Idea 4 keep her informed and get her back in. Two ideas from the left-field round are now part of it: every place shows a named person who looks after its volunteers (from Join Deb's Crew), and the first question is "Who are you here for?" (Follow My Runner). Build that as our own lean web app, so we keep control of the sign-in and of where details are stored, then add race-day tools and the passport once the basics are proven. What we build first

What Phase 1 looks like

One sign-up page for volunteers and one roster page for the team, proven at a small Running Wild event. There is a clickable example to try on your phone: open the Phase 1 example page. It is set at the Mt Buller SkyRun. The places, races and start times are real. The names, jobs and numbers are examples.

What a volunteer does

  • Who are you here for? If someone they know is running, they give a first name and the race. This step is optional.
  • When are you free? One tap.
  • What sort of job sounds good? Sitting down, on my feet, chatting with runners, somewhere quiet, or put me where I'm needed.
  • Where would you like to help? Three matched jobs, each titled by its place and job, with the time and the places left. Where it fits, a line says when they will see their runner.
  • A named person on every card. If the Aid Station Manager or contact for that place is confirmed, their face and hello show. If not, the Volunteer Coordinator looks after the volunteer until one is, and a text goes out when it is settled.
  • "I could lead this." A place with nobody leading it invites experienced volunteers to put their hand up. Organisers confirm who leads.
  • List or map. The same jobs on a sketch map of the course. Tap a place to see who works there. The list stays for anyone who would rather read than look at a map.
  • Name and mobile. Plus a contact for the day. No password.
  • You're in. A summary on screen and by text, a reminder the day before, and one tap to swap or pull out.
  • Anyone can skip the questions and browse every job, or ring and have an organiser sign them up.

What the team gets

  • Set up an event with its places, jobs, times and numbers needed. Nobody has to be named as leading a place on day one.
  • Add an Aid Station Manager or contact to a place whenever they are confirmed, with a photo and a hello. See who has offered to lead.
  • A live roster with the biggest gaps at the top.
  • Add a volunteer by phone on their behalf.
  • Message the volunteers at one place, or everyone, by text and email.
  • Automatic confirmations and reminders.
  • A printable roster for each place.
  • Past volunteers' contact details brought across from iVolunteer, with an invitation and an opt-out.

Held for later phases

  • Race-day check-in and reporting (Phase 2).
  • Volunteer Passport stamps, rewards and team sign-ups (Phase 3).

To settle while building: a sketch map and rough runner timings for each volunteer location on the pilot course, and each Aid Station Manager's or contact's consent to have their photo shown.
